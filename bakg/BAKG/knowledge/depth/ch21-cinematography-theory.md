The composition question set (run per shot):  (1) what is the eye's first stop? (2) is there a
competing second stop? (3) do lines/contrast/gaze support the subject? (4) is there depth (fg/mid/
bg)? (5) does framing serve the beat (D106) or fight it? (6) does the motion (subject and camera)
keep the composition alive?
21.3 CONTINUITY & THE EDITING RULES (D103
expanded)
The 180° rule:  keep the camera on one side of the action line; every shot's screen direction must
match the previous shot's implication. Break it deliberately (a 360° orbit, a character "crossing the
line" to signify power shift). Screen direction: a character moving screen-left must keep moving left
across cuts (unless the story shows a turn); entering/exiting frame: exit left → enter right (or cut to
the destination). Eyeline match: a character looking screen-left must be answered by what they see
screen-right; mismatched eyelines = broken dialogue. Match on action: cut during the motion (not
before, not after the pause) — the motion masks the cut; the classic invisible cut. Cut on motion /
cut on beat:  cutting as the subject moves or the music hits (D133) — rhythmic editing. The 30°
rule (classic): a cut between shots of the same subject should change angle by ≥30° or change size
— otherwise it's a "jump cut." (Jump cuts are now a  style — use deliberately.)  Insert discipline:
insert shots must match the master's screen direction and eyeline; the classic error: insert of a hand
with the wrong side.  Reaction shots: cut to the  reactor (the listener), not always the speaker —
reactions are where emotion lands; in comedy, the reaction is the punchline.
21.4 PACING THEORY (making rhythm intentional)
Shot duration vocabulary: 0.5–1 s = impact/subliminal; 1–3 s = action/normal; 3–6 s =
dialogue/breathing; 6–10 s = tension/landscape; 10+ s = meditation/weight. Duration = meaning:
short cuts = urgency/chaos; long cuts = calm/oppression.

Beats per minute: a fast film ~ 30–60 cuts/min; a slow film ~ 8–15. The variation matters more
than the average: a fast action beat followed by a long quiet hold = the classic rhythm shape
(crash → silence).
The "wave" pattern: build tension (shorter cuts, louder, faster motion) → release (long hold,
quiet) → repeat. Every film — even a 10-second loop — should have a micro version of this.
Pacing vs story: the emotional rhythm should lead the visual rhythm: sad moments get room,
jokes get snap, action gets breathlessness. An edit that follows the emotion feels right even when
"rules" say otherwise.

The rule:  transitions are  meaning, not decoration. The dissolve is a sentence; the hard cut is a
period; the smash cut is a scream. Use them sparingly and deliberately.
21.6 SOUND-IMAGE SYNCHRONIZATION (preview of Ch
23)
J/L cuts: the audio of the next scene starts while the picture is still on the current scene (J-cut —
audio leads) or the current audio carries into the next picture (L-cut) — the #1 pro technique for
smooth dialogue scenes.
Cutting to music: cut on the beat for energy, on the phrase for flow; stingers (accent hits) on
emotional beats.
Cutting to sound effects: the impact frame lands on the hit sound; anticipation on the wind-up
sound.
The animation→sound contract (D10/D120): the animatic's timing is the contract — final
animation must match the animatic's audio sync points frame-for-frame.
